<ShortAnswer>
Unused AI licences are almost never a tool problem. People don't use ChatGPT or Copilot because nobody showed them **which of their own tasks** it helps with, **what they're allowed to put into it**, and **what good output looks like**. Fix those three things and usage follows.
</ShortAnswer>

## The pattern I keep seeing

A business buys licences for the whole team. There's an email announcing it, maybe a lunchtime demo. For a week or two people try it: a poem for someone's birthday, a summary of a long email. Then usage drops off, and a few months later someone asks whether the subscription is worth renewing.

The people who kept using it usually worked out a use for it on their own. Everyone else didn't, and nobody followed up.

## Why people stop

<Pitfalls>
  <Pitfall title="They don't know what it's for.">"Use AI to be more productive" isn't a task. Nobody wakes up wanting to be more productive in general.</Pitfall>
  <Pitfall title="They're not sure what's allowed.">Without clear rules, careful people stay away from it, which is the right instinct and the wrong outcome.</Pitfall>
  <Pitfall title="The first answer was poor.">Most people try once, get something generic, and conclude it doesn't work for their kind of job.</Pitfall>
  <Pitfall title="It sits outside their tools.">If it means opening another tab and copying text back and forth, it loses to habit.</Pitfall>
</Pitfalls>

## What works instead

Start from the work. Sit with each team for an hour and list the tasks they repeat every week. Pick two or three that involve writing, reading documents or answering questions, then show people how to do *those* tasks with the tool, using their own documents.

Then write down the rules on a single page: what can go in, what can't, and who to ask. (There's a separate guide on [writing an AI usage policy](/blog/ai-usage-policy).)
