<ShortAnswer>
Unused AI licences are almost never a tool problem. People don't use ChatGPT or Copilot because nobody showed them **which of their own tasks** it helps with, **what they're allowed to put into it**, and **what good output looks like**. Fix those three things and usage follows.
</ShortAnswer>

## The pattern I keep seeing

A business buys licences for the whole team. There's an email announcing it, maybe a lunchtime demo. For a week or two people try it: a poem for someone's birthday, a summary of a long email. Then usage drops off, and a few months later someone asks whether the subscription is worth renewing.

The people who kept using it usually worked out a use for it on their own. Everyone else didn't, and nobody followed up.

In South Africa there's an extra sting. Most AI subscriptions are billed in dollars, so a licence that cost one amount in January can cost noticeably more by the time the annual renewal arrives. Paying more each year for something few people open is an easy line to cut, and often the wrong one, because the problem was never the tool.

## How to tell if you have this problem

Before you decide anything, look at the numbers. Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace admin centres show who has used Copilot or Gemini and how often. ChatGPT and Claude business plans have similar admin views. You're looking for three groups:

- people who use it most working days
- people who tried it a few times and stopped
- people who have never opened it

In most businesses, the first group is small and enthusiastic, the second is the biggest, and the third is bigger than the owner expects. The second group is where the opportunity is. They were willing, and something stopped them.

The second reason deserves more attention than it gets. Your most careful staff, the ones who handle client files, payroll or medical aid details, are often the ones who stop first, because they suspect POPIA applies and nobody has told them where the line is. They're right that it applies. Clear rules give them permission to use the tool for the work that's safe.

## A worked example

Picture a made-up 30-person accounting and payroll bureau in Pretoria that bought 30 business licences a year ago. The admin view shows 6 people using it weekly, 14 who tried it and stopped, and 10 who never logged in.

Talking to the 14 who stopped turns up the same few stories. A payroll administrator wasn't sure she could paste employee details in, so she didn't. A junior accountant asked it a tax question, got a confident answer that was wrong for South African rules, and stopped trusting it. Two client managers found it useful for emails but forgot it existed after the training lunch.

The fix in a case like this doesn't involve a new tool. Each team picks one real task. For client managers it's drafting the monthly "documents we still need from you" email. For payroll it's summarising policy changes into plain-English staff notices with no personal information in them. For the accountants it's rewording engagement letters, plus an explicit rule that tax answers always get checked against SARS guidance.

After a month, weekly users go from 6 to 18. The business also cuts 8 licences from people who will never need one, and that saving more than covers the time spent on training.

## What works instead

Start from the work. Sit with each team for an hour and list the tasks they repeat every week. Pick two or three that involve writing, reading documents or answering questions, then show people how to do _those_ tasks with the tool, using their own documents.

Show what good output looks like, too. Give people a short prompt they can copy for each task, with the context the tool needs: who the email is for, what tone to use, what to leave out. A first answer that's 80% right is a much better advert for the tool than a demo.

Then write down the rules on a single page: what can go in, what can't, and who to ask. There's a separate guide on [writing an AI usage policy](/blog/ai-usage-policy), and a free [template](/resources/ai-usage-policy-template) you can fill in.

Finally, name one person per team who collects questions and shares what works. It doesn't need to be a technical person. It needs to be someone the team asks for help anyway.

## Should you cancel?

Sometimes, yes. If a person's work doesn't involve much writing, reading or answering questions, they may never need a paid licence, and the free tier or no AI at all is fine. Cancelling those seats is sensible.

What I'd avoid is cancelling everything after one quiet quarter. Usually the people who never got shown a use for it are the ones who would have saved the most time. Run the two-week reset first, then decide seat by seat with the usage report in front of you.
