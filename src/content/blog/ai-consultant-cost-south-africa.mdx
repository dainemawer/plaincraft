## Why prices are hard to find

Most consultants don't publish prices. Some of that is habit: consulting has always been sold on proposals. Some of it is that "AI consulting" covers very different work, from a one-hour chat to a six-month software project, and a single number would mislead.

That leaves small businesses guessing, and guessing usually goes one of two ways. Either the owner assumes it's unaffordable and never asks, or they get a proposal with a total at the bottom and no way to tell whether it's fair.

So instead of a market average I can't stand behind, here are the ways consultants charge, what drives the price, what my own work costs and what you'll spend that isn't on the invoice.

## The four ways consultants charge

<NumberedList>
  <NumberedItem title="By the hour or day.">Simple, and fine for advice. The risk sits with you: if the work takes longer, you pay more. Ask for an estimate in hours before you start, and a cap.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="A fixed fee for a defined piece of work.">You know the cost up front, and the consultant carries the risk of it taking longer. The scope must be written down, so both sides know what's included.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="A monthly retainer.">A set number of days or hours a month for ongoing work, such as looking after automations or leading adoption. Good once you know what you need. Expensive if you sign one before you do.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="Free, paid for by someone else.">Software resellers and vendors often include "AI consulting" with a licence. It isn't wrong, but their advice tends to end with their product. Know who is paying.</NumberedItem>
</NumberedList>

For a small business trying AI for the first time, a fixed fee for a small, defined first step is usually the safest. You find out whether the consultant is any good, and whether AI helps your business, before committing to anything bigger.

## What drives the price

Five things move the number more than anything else.

**Size of the business.** An audit of a 15-person business means a handful of conversations. An audit of a 150-person business means several departments, more systems and more people to interview. That's why my audit is priced by headcount.

**Building versus advising.** Telling you where AI would help is quicker than building it. Automation that connects to your systems, handles errors and is documented so your team owns it is real software work.

**The state of your data.** If your price list lives in three spreadsheets and someone's head, that gets fixed first. It's often the most valuable part of the project, and it takes time.

**Governance.** If personal information is involved, and in most businesses it is, the work includes checking tools against POPIA, writing rules for staff and recording what goes where. Skipping it is cheaper until something goes wrong.

**Who does the work.** At larger firms, the senior person often sells the project and a junior team delivers it. Ask who will actually do the work, and what they've done before.

## The costs that aren't on the invoice

A consultant's fee is often not the biggest cost of an AI project. Budget for three others.

<Pitfalls>
  <Pitfall title="Licences.">Business plans for tools such as ChatGPT or Microsoft 365 Copilot are charged per user per month, usually in US dollars. Buy seats for the people whose work is changing, not the whole company.</Pitfall>
  <Pitfall title="Your team's time.">The person who knows the task best needs a few hours a week for a month: explaining how it works now, testing drafts and deciding what good enough looks like. Nobody else can do that part.</Pitfall>
  <Pitfall title="Looking after it.">Automations break when a supplier changes an invoice layout or a tool updates. Someone has to notice and fix it, either your team or a care plan.</Pitfall>
</Pitfalls>

A useful rule: if a proposal doesn't mention any of these, ask. A good consultant will have thought about them.

## A worked example

Picture a made-up engineering supplier in Durban with 40 staff. The owner suspects the sales team spends too long on quotes and wants to know whether AI would help.

The first step is an audit, at R65,000 for a business of that size. Over two weeks it finds that quotes are the biggest opportunity, at about 25 hours a week across the team. Two other ideas the owner had in mind turn out to be poor fits.

The quoting automation is then a fixed-fee build from R60,000, plus licences for the six people who quote. The sales manager spends about three hours a week for a month testing drafts against real requests.

If the build saves even half of those 25 hours, the business gets about 12 hours a week back. At a loaded cost of a few hundred rand an hour for sales staff, it pays for itself within months, and it keeps saving. If the audit had found nothing worth building, the owner would have spent R65,000 to learn that, rather than more on a project that didn't pay back.

## Questions to ask before you sign

Whoever you work with, these questions will tell you a lot:

<Checklist>
  <li>Is the fee fixed, and what exactly does it include?</li>
  <li>Who will do the work, and what have they built before?</li>
  <li>Do you take referral fees or commission from any software you might recommend?</li>
  <li>What will I own at the end: code, prompts, documentation, accounts?</li>
  <li>What will it cost to run each month after you've finished?</li>
  <li>How will you handle personal information under POPIA?</li>
</Checklist>

The third question matters more than it looks. A consultant paid by a vendor has a reason to recommend that vendor. I take no referral fees from software companies, and I'd ask the same of anyone you're considering.
