<ShortAnswer>
A small business should keep AI away from work that depends on **judgement**, **accountability** or **relationships**: hiring and firing, pricing strategy, difficult client conversations and anything where a person has to stand behind the decision. AI can help prepare for that work. It shouldn't do it.
</ShortAnswer>

## Why this list matters

Most writing about AI is about where to use it. In a small business, knowing where not to use it matters just as much, because one bad decision made by a tool can cost a client, an employee's trust or a complaint to a regulator. Those costs are much bigger than the hours AI saves elsewhere.

The work to keep for people tends to share three features. It needs judgement about things that aren't written down. Someone has to be accountable for the outcome. Or the relationship with the person on the other side is the point of the work.

## Judgement

AI produces plausible answers, which isn't the same as good judgement. When a decision weighs things that aren't written down anywhere, like a client's history, a team's morale or the risk you're willing to take, it needs a person who understands the context.

Pricing is a good example. AI can tell you what competitors charge, summarise your margins and model three scenarios. It can't know that your biggest client is about to go out to tender, that your best technician is thinking of leaving, or that you'd rather lose a job than win it at a loss. A person who knows the business makes that call.

The same goes for strategy more broadly. Use AI to gather information and test your thinking. Be wary of any tool that offers to make the decision for you.

## Accountability

If something goes wrong, someone has to explain why the decision was made. "The AI suggested it" isn't an explanation a client, employee or regulator will accept.

Hiring decisions are the clearest example: they affect people's livelihoods, and they need a person who can account for them. South African law reinforces this. The Employment Equity Act prohibits unfair discrimination in hiring, and an AI tool trained on past hiring data can repeat old patterns without anyone noticing. POPIA adds a specific rule: section 71 limits decisions with legal consequences, or that substantially affect a person, when they're based solely on automated processing. A person needs to be part of the decision for real, which means more than clicking "approve" on whatever the tool recommends.

The same applies to performance reviews, disciplinary steps, credit decisions about customers and anything that changes what an individual pays or receives.

## Relationships

Clients and staff can tell when a message was written to them and when it was generated for them. Use AI to draft routine replies. Write the difficult ones yourself.

That includes a complaint from a long-standing client, condolences to a staff member, news that a project is late, a retrenchment conversation and the first message to someone you want to work with. These are the moments people remember, and a generic, polished reply makes them feel processed.

> Use AI to prepare for the decision. Make the decision yourself.

## A worked example

Picture a made-up recruitment-heavy business: a 60-person security services company in Gauteng that hires guards every month. The operations manager is drowning in CVs and wants AI to shortlist applicants automatically.

The safe version looks different from the original idea. AI reads each CV and pulls out the facts that are pass-or-fail for the role: PSIRA registration grade, a valid driver's licence where needed, and availability for night shifts. Those are written-down requirements, the same for every applicant, and a person can check them quickly.

AI doesn't rank candidates or score "fit", and it never sees ID numbers, photos, or anything that hints at race, age or gender. A person reads the shortlist, interviews and decides. Rejected applicants are rejected by a person, who can say why.

The manager still saves most of the time spent sorting CVs, and the business can explain every hiring decision if it's ever asked to.

## The grey areas

Some work sits between the clear yes and the clear no. Three come up often.

Customer service chatbots can answer routine questions well: opening hours, order status, how to book. They become risky when they start making promises, handling complaints or giving advice that a customer will act on. Give the bot a short list of topics it may answer, and a quick route to a person for everything else.

Contracts and legal documents are another. AI is useful for summarising a long contract or spotting clauses you'd want to ask about. It isn't a substitute for a lawyer when the contract matters, and its summary can miss the one clause that does.

Marketing content is the third. AI drafts are fine for routine posts and product descriptions. Anything that makes claims about results, prices or comparisons with competitors needs a person who knows it's true, because your business is responsible for what it publishes, whoever wrote it.

## Where it still helps

<Checklist>
  <li>Summarising the background before a hard conversation.</li>
  <li>Drafting interview questions, then deciding with people.</li>
  <li>Pulling together the numbers before a pricing decision.</li>
  <li>Checking a difficult email you wrote yourself for tone, before you send it.</li>
  <li>Turning your notes from a meeting into a clear summary that you then edit.</li>
</Checklist>

## Putting the line in writing

The simplest way to make this stick is to write it into your AI usage policy: AI can help prepare, and a person decides on anything about a person. The free [AI usage policy template](/resources/ai-usage-policy-template) includes a section on exactly this, and the [POPIA + AI checklist](/resources/popia-ai-checklist) covers the automated-decision rule.

Part of every [readiness audit](/services/ai-readiness-audit) is a clear list of what to leave alone. Knowing where not to use AI is as useful as knowing where to use it.
