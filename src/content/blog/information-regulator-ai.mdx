## There's no AI law, but there is a regulator

People often assume that because South Africa hasn't passed an AI act, AI is unregulated. It isn't. The Protection of Personal Information Act has been fully in force since July 2021, and it applies to any processing of personal information, by any technology. An AI tool is just another way of processing it.

National AI policy has been under discussion for a while, and a draft was published in 2026. Policy direction may change, but POPIA is the law that applies today, and it's what the Information Regulator investigates and enforces. The Regulator can issue enforcement notices and administrative fines of up to R10 million, and some offences under the Act carry criminal penalties.

So the useful question for a small business isn't "what does the AI law say?" It's "what does POPIA expect when we use AI?"

## What the Regulator expects, in eight points

<NumberedList>
  <NumberedItem title="Someone is accountable.">The business, as the responsible party, is accountable for how personal information is processed, including by AI tools (section 8). In practice that means a named Information Officer, registered with the Regulator. In many small businesses it's the owner.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="You only use what you need.">Processing must be adequate, relevant and not excessive (section 10). If a task works with names removed, remove them.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="You use it for the purpose you collected it for.">Information collected to deliver a service shouldn't be fed into an AI tool for an unrelated purpose without thinking about whether that's compatible (sections 13 and 15).</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="People know.">When you collect personal information, people should know what it's for and who it may go to (section 18). If client data goes through AI tools, your privacy notice should say so.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="It's kept secure.">You must take reasonable technical and organisational measures to protect personal information (section 19). For AI, that means business-grade tools, access controls and staff rules.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="Providers are under contract.">An AI provider that processes personal information for you is an operator. You need a written contract requiring it to keep the information secure (sections 20 and 21).</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="Cross-border transfers are covered.">Most AI tools process data outside South Africa. Section 72 allows that only in certain conditions, such as the recipient being bound by comparable protection.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="Breaches are reported.">If personal information is compromised, you must notify the Regulator and the people affected as soon as reasonably possible (section 22). That includes a staff member pasting client files into the wrong tool.</NumberedItem>
</NumberedList>

None of this is AI-specific. That's the point. If your business already handles personal information properly in email and accounting software, AI tools are an extension of the same habits.

## The two areas where AI is different

Two parts of POPIA matter more for AI than for most other software.

### Automated decisions about people

Section 71 says a person may not be subject to a decision that has legal consequences for them, or affects them to a substantial degree, based solely on automated processing that builds a profile of them. Think creditworthiness, reliability, performance at work or health. There are exceptions, for example where the decision relates to a contract and the person's interests are protected, but they come with conditions, such as letting the person make representations.

For a small business, the practical rule is simple: AI can gather information and suggest. A person decides anything that significantly affects an individual, and can explain why. Screening CVs, scoring staff, deciding who gets credit or flagging a customer as high-risk all need a human in the loop. [Where AI shouldn't go](/blog/where-ai-shouldnt-go) covers this in more depth.

### Special personal information

Health, religion, race, biometrics, criminal behaviour and the other special categories in section 26 can generally only be processed under specific conditions. Information about children has its own rules too. These categories should stay out of general AI tools unless you've checked you're allowed to put them there and the tool is approved for it.

## A worked example

Picture a made-up recruitment agency in Johannesburg with 18 staff. Consultants have started pasting CVs into a free AI chatbot to write candidate summaries for clients, and one has started asking it to rank candidates for each role.

Working through the points above, the owner finds four problems. CVs contain personal information and sometimes special personal information, such as health details or race for employment equity purposes. The free accounts have no contract, may store data abroad and may use it for training. Candidates weren't told their CVs might go through AI tools. And ranking candidates by AI comes close to the kind of solely automated decision section 71 is about.

The fixes are straightforward. The agency moves to a business plan with a data processing agreement and no training on its data. It updates its privacy notice for candidates. Summaries are still drafted with AI, but special personal information is removed first and a consultant checks every summary. Ranking stays with consultants, who can use AI to compare CVs against the brief but make and explain the shortlist themselves. The owner, as Information Officer, writes the rules down and briefs the team.

The agency keeps most of the time saving, and if the Regulator or a candidate ever asks how it uses AI, it has an answer.

## What it doesn't expect

It's easy to read all of this and conclude that AI is too risky to touch. That's not what POPIA says, and it's not a sensible reading of how the Regulator works.

POPIA asks for *reasonable* measures, proportionate to the information and the risk. A 15-person business isn't expected to have a bank's compliance department. It is expected to have thought about what it's doing, written down a few rules, used tools with proper agreements and acted quickly if something went wrong.

Nor does the Act require you to avoid processing information outside South Africa, or to stop using AI for anything that touches personal information. It sets conditions. Most of the large AI providers publish terms designed to meet conditions like these on their business plans. Your job is to be on those plans, keep a copy of the terms and check them now and then.

The businesses most likely to have a problem aren't the ones using AI. They're the ones whose staff are using it without anyone knowing.

If the Regulator, a client or a candidate asked how your business uses AI with personal information, could you show them these?

<Checklist>
  <li>A list of the AI tools staff use, and which plan each is on.</li>
  <li>A written agreement with each provider that processes personal information for you.</li>
  <li>A short AI usage policy that says what may and may not go in.</li>
  <li>A privacy notice that mentions AI tools where they're used.</li>
  <li>A named, registered Information Officer.</li>
  <li>A note of any decisions about people that AI contributes to, and who makes the final call.</li>
