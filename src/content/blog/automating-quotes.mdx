## Where the afternoon goes

Watch someone prepare a quote and the time rarely goes on the thinking. It goes on finding the last similar quote, copying line items, checking the current price list, rewording the scope and fixing the formatting. That's exactly the kind of work AI does well.

It also matters more than it looks. In most trades and service businesses, the quote that arrives first has a better chance of winning, and a quote that takes three days to send tells the client something about how the job will go. Faster quotes are an admin saving and a sales tool at the same time.

## Is your quoting a good fit?

Quotes are a strong candidate for AI when most of these are true:

<Checklist>
  <li>You send more than about ten quotes a week, or each one takes over 30 minutes.</li>
  <li>Most quotes are variations on a handful of standard jobs.</li>
  <li>Prices come from a list, a spreadsheet or a few clear rules.</li>
  <li>Requests arrive in writing: email, a web form or WhatsApp.</li>
  <li>Someone experienced can check a draft in a few minutes.</li>
</Checklist>

If every quote is a one-off that needs a site visit and an engineer's judgement, AI can still help with the write-up, but the saving is smaller. Start with the common jobs.

## Step by step

<NumberedList>
  <NumberedItem title="Write down how quotes are built today.">The inputs, the price list, the rules ("travel over 50 km is charged", "add 15% VAT", "minimum call-out fee"), and who signs off.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="Collect ten good past quotes.">These become the examples the AI follows for tone, structure and level of detail. Pick ones you'd be happy to send again.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="Put the price list somewhere the tool can read it.">One current, tidy source. If prices live in three places, fix that first.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="Build the draft step.">The request goes in, a structured draft comes out with each line item linked to the price list, and anything the tool isn't sure about is flagged.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="Keep a person in the loop.">They check quantities, prices and scope before anything reaches a client.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="Measure it.">Time per quote before and after, how often the draft needs big changes, and your win rate.</NumberedItem>
</NumberedList>

The first three steps are where most of the effort goes, and they're useful even if you never automate anything. A written-down quoting process and a single price list make it easier to train new staff and harder to undercharge.

## A worked example

Take a made-up refrigeration and air-conditioning contractor in Durban with 18 staff. Two office administrators prepare about 25 quotes a week for maintenance contracts, repairs and new installations. Each quote takes around 45 minutes, so quoting uses close to 19 hours a week.

Requests come in by email and WhatsApp, often with photos and model numbers. Prices sit in a spreadsheet the owner updates twice a year, with a few rules nobody has written down: after-hours rates, a travel charge outside the eThekwini metro, and a discount for existing maintenance clients.

The project starts by writing those rules down and moving the price list into one shared spreadsheet. The AI step then reads each request, identifies the job type, pulls the matching line items and writes a draft in the company's usual wording. Anything unusual, such as a unit it doesn't recognise or a site far outside the normal area, gets flagged for a person.

In a setup like this, a draft might take the administrator 10 to 15 minutes to check and adjust, down from 45. That's 12 hours a week or more back, which can go on following up open quotes. The owner keeps signing off anything over an agreed amount, as before.

## Which tools?

For most small businesses the first version runs on what you already have. If you use Microsoft 365, Copilot in Word and Excel can draft from a template and a price list. If you use Google Workspace, Gemini does the same in Docs and Sheets. A ChatGPT or Claude business plan works too, with a saved set of instructions and examples.

If your quoting already happens inside job-management or accounting software, check what AI features it includes before building anything separate. Many now draft quotes or descriptions for you. The right answer is often the tool your team opens every day anyway.

A more automated version, where requests flow in from email or a web form and drafts appear in a shared folder for checking, is worth building once the simple version has proved its value.

## What it costs

The tool cost is usually small: a few business licences for the people who quote, which you may already have. The bigger cost is the setup time. Writing down the rules, cleaning the price list and testing the drafts takes a few days of work from the person who knows quoting best, spread over two or three weeks.

To decide whether it's worth it, do the sum before you start. Multiply the number of quotes a week by the minutes you expect to save on each, then by what an hour of that person's time costs you. In the Durban example, 12 hours a week at R250 an hour is about R13,000 a month. If your number is a few hundred rand, quoting isn't your first project, and that's useful to know too.

Keep one more number in mind: your win rate. If faster, more consistent quotes win even one extra job a month, that often matters more than the hours.

## What to watch for

<Pitfalls>
  <Pitfall title="Messy pricing.">The AI will be as consistent as your price list, and no more.</Pitfall>
  <Pitfall title="Skipping the check.">A wrong price sent to a client costs more than the time saved.</Pitfall>
  <Pitfall title="Over-building.">Start with the common quotes. Leave the unusual ones to a person.</Pitfall>
  <Pitfall title="Client details in the wrong tool.">Names, phone numbers and addresses in a quote request are personal information under POPIA. Use a business plan that doesn't train on your data, and check where it stores it.</Pitfall>
</Pitfalls>
