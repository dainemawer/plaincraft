The five jobs worth automating first in most accounting and bookkeeping practices are **capturing bills and receipts**, **chasing missing documents**, **drafting client emails**, **month-end commentary** and **answering staff questions from your own procedures**. None of them involve AI making professional judgements. They're the admin around the work, and that's where most of the hours go.

## Why practices are a good fit

Accounting practices are one of the easiest kinds of business to help with AI, for three reasons.

First, the work is repetitive in the right way. The same documents arrive every month from the same clients, in roughly the same shapes. Second, most practices already moved to cloud ledgers such as Xero, Sage or QuickBooks, so the data is in one place and the tools have connections built in. Third, there's a professional checking every output already. Reviewing an AI draft fits a workflow that's built on review.

The risk is the opposite of most businesses. Practices tend to buy the tools, such as capture software or a Copilot licence, and use a fraction of them, because nobody had time to set them up properly during tax season.

## The five jobs

<NumberedList>
  <NumberedItem title="Capturing bills and receipts.">AI reads supplier, dates, totals, VAT and line items from PDFs and photos, matches them to the right supplier and account, and prepares them in the ledger for someone to approve. The [invoice capture guide](/blog/invoice-capture) walks through it in detail.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="Chasing missing documents.">At year end, much of a practice's time goes on reminding clients to send bank statements, IRP5s, medical aid certificates and supporting documents. AI drafts a personalised reminder for each client from your checklist of what's still outstanding. Staff review and send.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="Drafting client emails.">Explaining a SARS letter, a provisional tax estimate or a change in the management accounts in plain English. You give it the facts and the point you want to make; it gives you a draft in your practice's tone.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="Month-end commentary.">A paragraph on what changed this month and why, from figures you already have. It turns a management pack into something clients actually read, without an extra hour per client.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="Answering staff questions.">An internal assistant that answers from your own procedures, checklists and past file notes. Juniors ask it how the practice handles a particular kind of journal before they interrupt a manager.</NumberedItem>
</NumberedList>

Notice what's not on the list: tax opinions, audit judgements, signing off financial statements, deciding how to treat a transaction. AI can help prepare for those, but the professional judgement and the signature stay with you.

## Where to start

Start with the job that costs your practice the most hours and has the most obvious check. For most bookkeeping-heavy practices that's capture. For practices with more advisory work it's often client emails or month-end commentary.

To pick, estimate each job with three numbers: how often it happens, how long it takes, and how long checking a good draft would take. The difference, multiplied across your client base, is the time on the table.

Then ask a second question: who would notice if it went wrong? Capture errors are caught at review. A badly worded client email is caught before it's sent. Pick jobs where the check is already part of how the practice works, and the first project will feel like a better version of what you do now, not a new risk.

<Pitfalls>
  <Pitfall title="Messy chart of accounts.">AI suggestions are only as consistent as the accounts and rules they work from. Tidy those first.</Pitfall>
  <Pitfall title="Automating during busy season.">Set things up in a quiet month. Nobody tests a new process properly in the last week of February.</Pitfall>
  <Pitfall title="Skipping the review.">AI capture gets most bills right. The ones it gets wrong are the reason a person still approves each one.</Pitfall>
  <Pitfall title="Client data in personal accounts.">Tax numbers, ID numbers and bank details belong in your ledger and capture tools, under a written agreement. Not in a chatbot on someone's personal login.</Pitfall>
</Pitfalls>

## Measuring whether it worked

Practices are good at measuring other people's businesses and often less good at measuring their own. Before you switch anything on, write down three numbers for the job you've chosen: how many times it happens a month, how long it takes now and how many errors or rework you see.

Then check the same three numbers after a month and again after three. Time per item is usually the obvious win. Errors are often the surprise: capture with a consistent set of supplier rules makes fewer typing mistakes than a tired person in February.

Ask the team as well. The people doing the work will tell you where the drafts are good, where they need too much fixing and where the process around the tool needs changing. That feedback is how a first automation turns into a second one.

Finally, decide what the time is for. Hours saved that quietly disappear into more of the same work don't change much. Hours that go into advisory conversations, or into taking on clients you'd otherwise have turned away, change the practice.

## Confidentiality and POPIA

Client financial records about individuals and sole proprietors are personal information under POPIA, and your professional body's code of conduct requires confidentiality on top of that. AI doesn't change those duties. It adds new places where information can leak.

Three rules cover most of it. Use business plans that don't train on your data, under a written agreement. Check where each tool stores data, because many process it outside South Africa and section 72 of POPIA sets the conditions for that. And give staff a one-page policy on what may go into which tool. The [POPIA checklist](/blog/popia-ai-tools-checklist) has the full list of questions.

It's also worth adding a line to your engagement letter saying which tools you use to process client records. It answers the question before a client asks it.

## A worked example

Picture a made-up bookkeeping practice in Stellenbosch with eight staff and 60 monthly clients, on Xero. The partners already pay for a capture tool but most bills still arrive by email and get typed in by hand, because nobody set up the forwarding addresses and supplier rules.

They start with capture. Over three weeks in a quiet month, each client gets a forwarding address, the 50 most common suppliers get rules, and the team agrees that anything the tool flags as uncertain gets a closer look. Typing time drops from about 3 minutes a bill to about 1 minute of review.

With roughly 1,500 bills a month across the client base, that's about 50 hours a month back. The partners spend part of it on the next job, year-end document chasing. A reminder template, filled in from each client's outstanding list, turns an afternoon of emails into an hour of reviewing and sending.

Neither change needed new software. Both needed someone to set them up properly and write down how they work.
