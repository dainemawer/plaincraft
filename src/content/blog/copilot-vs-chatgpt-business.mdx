## Two different kinds of tool

The comparison is usually framed as which AI is smarter. That's the wrong question. Both use capable models, and both are good at drafting, summarising and answering questions. The real difference is where they live.

**Microsoft 365 Copilot** is built into the Microsoft apps your team already has open. It can draft a reply in Outlook from the email thread, summarise a Teams meeting you missed, or pull figures from a spreadsheet into a Word report. It works from your business's emails, files and meetings, within each person's existing permissions.

**ChatGPT Business** (previously ChatGPT Team) is a separate workspace. People go to it to write, think, analyse a file or research a question. It can connect to tools such as SharePoint, Google Drive and email, but its home is its own window, not inside your documents.

So the choice depends less on the AI and more on how your team works.

## What each costs

Both are priced per user per month, in US dollars, excluding VAT. Prices change often, so treat these as the list prices at the time of writing and check before you buy:

- **Microsoft 365 Copilot Business** is an add-on for businesses with up to 300 users, at about US$21 per user per month on an annual commitment. You also need an eligible Microsoft 365 business plan, which you probably already have.
- **ChatGPT Business** is about US$25 per user per month billed monthly, and less on an annual plan.

At those prices, licensing all 30 people costs roughly US$630 to US$750 a month, before VAT and exchange rate movements. Licensing the eight people who'd use it daily costs about a quarter of that.

One more thing worth knowing: Microsoft 365 business plans include a free Copilot Chat, a web-based assistant with enterprise data protection, but without the full Copilot's access to your emails and files. For some staff, that's enough.

## Where Copilot wins

Copilot is strongest when the work is spread across your Microsoft 365 data.

- **Email-heavy roles.** Drafting replies, summarising long threads and catching up after leave.
- **Meetings.** Summaries, action items and "what did I miss?" for meetings recorded in Teams.
- **Finding things.** Asking where the latest version of a proposal is, or what was agreed with a client, across email, chat and files.
- **Office documents.** First drafts in Word from existing files, and help with formulas and summaries in Excel.

It also sits inside the admin, security and compliance tools your Microsoft 365 administrator already manages, which is a real advantage for governance.

The catch is that Copilot can only find what people already have access to. If your SharePoint permissions are a mess, with everyone able to open payroll, Copilot will make that obvious very quickly. Fix permissions before you switch it on.

## Where ChatGPT Business wins

ChatGPT Business is strongest as a general-purpose assistant.

- **Writing from scratch.** Proposals, marketing copy, policies and difficult emails, where you're starting from a brief rather than an existing thread.
- **Thinking work.** Working through a problem, comparing options or preparing for a negotiation.
- **Analysing files.** Uploading a spreadsheet or a long PDF and asking questions of it.
- **Custom assistants.** Shared GPTs with your instructions and reference documents for repeated tasks, such as a quote drafter or a tone-of-voice checker.

It's also the natural choice if you run on Google Workspace, where Copilot isn't an option.

Both business plans say they don't train on your business data by default, and both have admin controls. Read the current terms for the plan you're actually buying, because free and personal plans work differently. The [POPIA checklist](/blog/popia-ai-tools-checklist) covers what to check.

## A 30-person business, worked through

Picture a made-up property management company in Pretoria with 30 staff, on Microsoft 365 Business Standard.

- **Eight portfolio managers** live in Outlook and Teams, answering landlords and tenants all day. Copilot drafts their replies from the thread and summarises their weekly meetings.
- **Three people in marketing and leasing** write listings, newsletters and letters from scratch. ChatGPT Business suits them better.
- **The finance team of four** want help with spreadsheets and reports. They trial Copilot for a month before deciding.
- **The other fifteen** use free Copilot Chat, with a one-page policy on what they can put into it.

That's 12 paid Copilot seats and 3 ChatGPT Business seats, not 30 of either. After three months, the usage reports show who's using what, and seats move accordingly.

## Setting it up properly

Whichever you choose, the setup matters more than the choice. Four things make the difference between a tool people use and a line on the bank statement.

**Permissions first.** For Copilot, check who can open what in SharePoint, OneDrive and Teams before you switch it on. Copilot respects existing permissions, so if a folder of salaries is open to everyone, anyone can now ask about it in plain English. For ChatGPT Business, decide which connectors to allow and who can create shared assistants.

**Rules in writing.** A one-page policy that says which tool is approved, what may go into it and what may not. Client ID numbers, health details and bank details are the usual exclusions. Without rules, your most careful staff won't use it at all.

**Training on real tasks.** An hour per team, on their own work, with prompts they can reuse. Generic "intro to AI" sessions are forgotten by Friday.

**A check after a month.** Both tools have admin reports showing who's using them. Look at them after four weeks and again after three months. Move seats to the people who use them, and ask the others what got in the way.

Run a short, honest trial before you buy for everyone.
