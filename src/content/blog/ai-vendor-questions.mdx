Before a South African business signs with any AI vendor, it should ask five questions: **where does our data go**, **is it used for training**, **what happens when we leave**, **what will it really cost at our size**, and **who do we call when it breaks**. Clear answers are a good sign. Vague ones are your answer.

## Why these five

AI products are easy to demo and hard to compare. The demo shows the best case. The contract decides what happens in every other case. These questions move the conversation from features to the things that cost money later.

They also work whatever you're buying: an AI assistant for the whole team, a chatbot for your website, a document-reading tool for the finance department, or an "AI-powered" upgrade to software you already use.

## What good and bad answers sound like

### Where does our data go?

A good answer names the regions where data is stored and processed, points you to a published list of sub-processors, and offers a data processing agreement. Some providers let you choose a region, which can make the POPIA conversation easier.

A weak answer is "it's all in the cloud and fully secure". That says nothing about where. Under section 72 of POPIA, sending personal information outside South Africa is allowed in certain conditions, such as the recipient being bound by comparable protection, and you can't check those conditions without knowing where the data goes.

### Is our data used for training?

A good answer is a clear no for your plan, in the contract or the published terms, with any exceptions spelled out (for example, if you choose to send feedback). A weak answer is "we take privacy very seriously", or a yes that you can switch off only by emailing support.

### What happens when we leave?

You want to hear that you can export your data, your settings and anything you've built, such as templates or a trained chatbot's knowledge base, in a standard format, and that deletion happens within a stated number of days. If the answer is that your setup can't be moved, you're buying lock-in along with the product.

### What will it cost at our size?

Ask for a written quote for your actual numbers: users, documents a month, conversations a month. Then ask what year two looks like. A lot of AI pricing has a low entry price, then charges per use, per "credit" or for features that turn out to be add-ons. Most international vendors bill in dollars or euros, so ask whether they invoice in rands and how they handle exchange rate changes on annual contracts.

### Who do we call when it breaks?

Support hours matter more than people expect. A US vendor whose support desk opens at 3pm South African time is fine for a nice-to-have tool and a real problem for something your customers rely on. Ask about response times, whether there's a local partner, and what happens if the AI gives a customer wrong information.

## A worked example

Picture a made-up Cape Town e-commerce business with 25 staff, selling outdoor gear. They're choosing between two AI chatbots for customer service, both demoed well and both promising to answer "where's my order?" questions automatically.

Vendor A is a local reseller of an international product. Asked the five questions, they send a data processing agreement, a sub-processor list showing EU hosting, a rand quote with a fixed price for 12 months, and support from a Johannesburg team during business hours. Export is a CSV of conversations and a copy of the knowledge base.

Vendor B is cheaper on paper. Their answers are a link to a general privacy page, "industry-standard security", a dollar price per conversation with no cap, and email support from another time zone. Asked twice about training, they say it's "configurable".

On a busy month, Vendor B's per-conversation pricing would cost more than Vendor A's fixed fee, before the exchange rate moves. The decision takes ten minutes once the answers are side by side. The demo didn't show any of this.

## Before you sign

Check whether a tool you already pay for, such as Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, your accounting software or your helpdesk, does most of the job. It often does, and the data questions are already answered because you've signed those terms.

Run a short trial on your own work before committing to an annual contract. Two weeks with real, anonymised examples tells you more than any demo.

And have one person own the decision. When five people each like a different demo, the loudest preference wins, and that's rarely the best fit.
