## Why POPIA matters for AI tools

The Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA) applies whenever your business processes personal information: names, ID numbers, contact details, health or financial information about clients, staff or suppliers. Pasting that information into an AI tool is processing it. So is uploading a spreadsheet, recording a meeting with an AI note-taker or connecting a tool to your inbox.

None of that is forbidden. It just needs the same care you'd give any other system that holds client data, such as your accounting software or CRM.

Two things make AI tools different from the software you're used to. First, staff can start using them in minutes, on free personal accounts, without anyone in the business knowing. Second, many tools send what you type to servers outside South Africa, and some free plans use it to improve their models. Neither is a problem if you've checked. Both are a problem if you haven't.

## The three questions people get wrong

Most of the checklist is common sense once it's written down. Three items catch small businesses out more than the rest.

### Cross-border transfers

Section 72 of POPIA limits sending personal information to another country. It's allowed when the recipient is bound by a law, binding rules or an agreement that gives protection comparable to POPIA, when the person consents, or in a few other situations the Act lists. The large AI providers publish data processing terms meant to cover this, but those terms usually apply to their business plans. A staff member using a free personal account is unlikely to be covered by anything your business has agreed to.

### Training on your data

"Does it train on our data?" has a different answer depending on the plan, and sometimes on a setting. Business and enterprise plans from the main providers generally don't train on your inputs by default. Free and personal plans often can, unless the user switches it off. Check the plan you're actually paying for, in writing, and check again when the provider changes its terms.

### Special personal information

POPIA treats some information as special: religious or philosophical beliefs, race or ethnic origin, trade union membership, political views, health, sex life, biometrics and criminal behaviour. Information about children has its own rules too. Processing these categories is generally prohibited unless an exception applies. For a small business the practical rule is simple: keep them out of general AI tools unless you've checked you're allowed to put them in.

## A worked example

Picture a made-up physiotherapy practice in Cape Town with 12 staff. The receptionist starts using a free AI chatbot to draft appointment reminder messages and to summarise referral letters from GPs, because it saves her an hour a day.

The reminders are fine once names are swapped for placeholders. The referral letters are a problem: they contain health information, which is special personal information, and she's pasting them into a free personal account that may store them abroad and use them for training.

Working through the checklist, the practice decides three things. Reminders can be drafted with AI using templates and no patient details. Referral summaries stay manual for now, until the practice has a business-grade tool with a proper agreement and the owner, who is the Information Officer, has checked the health-information rules. The receptionist gets a company account and a one-page policy that says all of this in plain words.

She keeps most of the time saving, and the practice no longer has patient files sitting in a stranger's chat history.
