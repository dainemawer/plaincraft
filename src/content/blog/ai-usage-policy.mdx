<ShortAnswer>
A useful AI usage policy for a small business fits on **one page**, uses **plain language**, and gives **real examples** from your business. It says which tools are approved, what information can and can't go in, when a person must check the output, and who to ask.
</ShortAnswer>

## Why most policies don't get read

They're too long, too legal or too vague. A ten-page document full of "users shall" gets signed and forgotten. People need something they can check in thirty seconds when they're about to paste something into a chatbot.

The other common failure is a policy that only says no. "Do not use AI tools without approval" sounds safe, but in practice staff carry on using free accounts quietly, because the work still has to get done. A policy that names the approved tools and the tasks they're good for gets followed, because following it is easier than working around it.

## Do you actually need one?

If anyone in your business uses ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini or any other AI tool for work, yes. That includes staff using free accounts on their phones. In South Africa there are two practical reasons.

The first is POPIA. Your business is responsible for the personal information it processes, including what staff type into AI tools. A written policy is one of the clearest ways to show you've taken reasonable steps to protect that information, and it tells staff where the line is.

The second is quality. AI tools produce confident mistakes, and a rule that says "a person checks anything that goes to a client" prevents most of the embarrassing ones.

## What to put on the page

<NumberedList>
  <NumberedItem title="Approved tools.">Name them, with the account to use. Anything not on the list needs a quick check first.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="What can go in.">Your own drafts, public information, anonymised examples.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="What can't go in.">ID numbers, health or financial details, client data the tool isn't approved for, passwords.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="When a person must check.">Anything sent to a client, anything with numbers, anything legal or HR.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="Who to ask.">One named person, not "management".</NumberedItem>
</NumberedList>

Two more sections are worth adding once the basics are in place. One covers decisions about people: AI can help prepare, but a person decides on hiring, discipline, credit or pricing for an individual. The other covers what to do when something goes wrong, so a staff member who pastes the wrong file into a chatbot reports it the same day instead of hoping nobody notices.

## A worked example

Take a made-up 40-person property management company in Johannesburg. Leasing agents use ChatGPT on personal accounts to write listing descriptions and tenant emails. The finance team doesn't use AI at all, because nobody has said whether they may.

The owner wants a policy by the end of the month. The first draft, adapted from a template a friend sent, runs to seven pages and nobody reads past the second.

The version that works fits on one page. It approves Microsoft Copilot, which the company already pays for through Microsoft 365, and asks staff to stop using personal ChatGPT accounts for work. It says listing descriptions, rewording emails and summarising lease clauses are fine. Tenant ID numbers, bank details and credit reports never go into any AI tool. Anything sent to a tenant or landlord gets read in full first. Questions go to the operations manager, by name.

The finance team gets one extra line: AI can help draft the cover notes for monthly owner statements, and the numbers always come from the accounting system, never from the AI.

The briefing takes 15 minutes in the Monday meeting, with two real examples per team. Everyone signs the last line.

## What to leave out

A short policy works because it leaves things out. Four things in particular don't belong on the page.

Definitions of AI and machine learning are the first. Your staff don't need to know how a language model works to follow a rule about ID numbers. If a term needs explaining, use an example instead.

The second is a list of every risk you can think of. Bias, hallucination, intellectual property and deepfakes are all real, but a list of fifteen risks reads as "don't use AI" and gets ignored. Turn the ones that matter to your business into a rule: "read every word before it goes to a client" covers hallucination better than a paragraph about it.

The third is copy-pasted legal wording. A clause from a large company's policy, written for its lawyers, will confuse a team of twenty. If you need formal terms for contractors or for your employment contracts, keep them in those documents and point to them.

The fourth is anything you won't enforce. A rule that says staff may never use AI on their phones, when everyone does, teaches people that the policy doesn't mean what it says. Write the rule you'll actually hold people to.

## Making it stick

Walk the team through it in fifteen minutes, with examples from their own work. Put the one page somewhere people will see it: pinned in Teams or Slack, printed next to the printer, or attached to the onboarding pack for new staff.

Review it every six months, or whenever you add a tool. AI products change their features and terms often, and a rule written for last year's version can quietly stop making sense.
