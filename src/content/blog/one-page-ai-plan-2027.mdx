<ShortAnswer>
You don't need an AI strategy document for 2027. You need **one page** with six things on it: **one goal**, **three tasks** to try AI on, **the rules** for data, **a budget**, **an owner** and **two review dates**. Fill it in over the December break, and you'll start the year with something specific instead of a vague intention.
</ShortAnswer>

## Why one page

Most small businesses ended 2026 in one of two places with AI. Some never started, because it all felt too big or too vague. Others bought licences, tried a few things and lost momentum when the year got busy.

Both problems have the same fix: a short, specific plan that fits on one page. Short enough that you'll actually write it. Specific enough that someone can act on it in January. And written down, so that in June you can check whether it happened.

It also makes conversations easier. When a vendor calls in February with a new AI tool, you can hold it up against the page: does this help with one of our three tasks? If not, it can wait. When a team member asks whether they can use a tool with client data, the rules are already written. One page, used properly, saves a lot of meetings.

A long strategy document isn't wrong, but for a business with 10 to 250 staff it's usually the wrong first step. It takes weeks, it's out of date by the time it's finished, and it rarely says what anyone should do on Monday.

## The six parts

<NumberedList>
  <NumberedItem title="One goal.">What do you want AI to change in your business by the end of 2027? Make it a business outcome, not an AI outcome. "Quotes go out the same day" beats "use AI more".</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="Three tasks.">The three repeated tasks you'll try AI on first. Good candidates involve writing, reading documents or answering routine questions, and have an obvious way for a person to check the result.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="The rules.">Which tools are approved, what information may go into them and what may not. Half a page is enough. The [AI usage policy guide](/blog/ai-usage-policy) shows how.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="A budget.">Tool licences for the people involved, plus any outside help. Write a number down, even a rough one, so you can tell whether it paid back.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="An owner.">One named person who's responsible for the plan happening. Not a committee.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="Two review dates.">One at the end of March to check the first task, one at the end of June to decide what's next. Put them in the calendar now.</NumberedItem>
</NumberedList>

## Choosing the three tasks

This is the part that matters most, so spend most of your time on it.

Ask each team for the three tasks they repeat most and like least. You'll get a long list. Then, for each one, estimate three numbers: how often it happens, how long it takes and how long it would take a person to check a good draft. Multiply out the difference, and sort.

The tasks at the top of the list, with a clear check, are your candidates. Pick three, and put them in order. You'll do the first one properly before you start the second.

<Pitfalls>
  <Pitfall title="Picking the exciting task over the valuable one.">A chatbot on the website is fun. Cutting quote turnaround from two days to two hours probably matters more.</Pitfall>
  <Pitfall title="Picking a task with no check.">If nobody can tell whether the AI got it right, it isn't a good first task.</Pitfall>
  <Pitfall title="Picking a decision about people.">Hiring, discipline and credit decisions aren't good first projects. [Where AI shouldn't go](/blog/where-ai-shouldnt-go) explains why.</Pitfall>
</Pitfalls>

## A worked example

Picture a made-up architecture practice in Cape Town with 22 staff. The partners have talked about AI all year without doing much. Over the December break, one partner fills in the page.

**Goal:** Spend less senior time on admin, so partners spend more time designing.

**Three tasks:** First, drafting fee proposals from the brief and past proposals, which takes a partner about four hours each and happens twice a week. Second, writing up site meeting minutes from notes. Third, answering contractors' routine questions about drawings.

**Rules:** ChatGPT Business for drafting, no client personal information beyond names and project addresses, partners check every proposal before it goes out.

**Budget:** Five ChatGPT Business seats for the year, plus a half-day training session for the team.

**Owner:** The partner who wrote the page.

**Reviews:** 31 March, to see whether proposal drafting saves time. 30 June, to decide whether to move on to meeting minutes.

The whole page took about two hours to write. In January, the practice starts on proposals. By March it knows whether it worked.

## A blank version to copy

Here's the page, ready to fill in. Copy it into a document, print it or write it on the back of an envelope. The format doesn't matter. Writing it down does.

<Checklist>
  <li><strong>Goal for 2027:</strong> what should be different by December, in business terms?</li>
  <li><strong>Task 1:</strong> what it is, how often it happens and how long it takes now.</li>
  <li><strong>Task 2:</strong> the same.</li>
  <li><strong>Task 3:</strong> the same.</li>
  <li><strong>Approved tools:</strong> which ones, on which plan, for whom.</li>
  <li><strong>Never goes in:</strong> the information that stays out of AI tools, such as ID numbers, health details and bank details.</li>
  <li><strong>Budget:</strong> licences plus any outside help, for the year.</li>
  <li><strong>Owner:</strong> one name.</li>
  <li><strong>Reviews:</strong> end of March, end of June.</li>
</Checklist>

A few tips for filling it in. Write the goal last, once you've seen the tasks, because the tasks usually tell you what the goal really is. Ask the people who do the work to estimate the time, not their managers. And keep the rules section short enough that you'd be happy to stick it on the kitchen wall. If a rule needs a paragraph to explain, it probably needs a conversation instead.

The December break is a good time for this for a simple reason: it's the one time of year most owners have a few quiet hours to think about how the business works, rather than working in it. Two hours in December is worth more than a full day in March.

## Then what?

Once the page is written, January is about the first task only. Set it up, train the people involved and use it on real work for a month. Measure the time it takes before and after. At the March review, decide honestly: keep it, fix it or drop it.

Most businesses that follow this find that the first task works, the second needs adjustment and the third changes once they've learned from the first two. That's fine. The page is a plan, not a contract.
