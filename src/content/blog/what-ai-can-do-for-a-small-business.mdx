## Start with the work, not the tool

Most owners I speak to start in the wrong place. They try a chatbot, get a mediocre answer and decide AI isn't for them. Or they buy licences for everyone and wait for something to happen.

A better question is simpler: _what does my team do every week that's repetitive, rule-based and boring?_ That list is where AI pays back, and the tool comes second.

In a 20-person business the list is usually longer than people expect. Someone retypes supplier invoices into Sage or Xero. Someone else writes the same five kinds of email to clients, with small changes each time. A sales person rebuilds a quote from an old one every Monday. A manager spends Friday afternoon turning WhatsApp messages and notes into a report nobody enjoys reading. None of this is skilled work, and all of it takes hours.

## The three jobs AI does well

Almost every useful AI project in a small business is a version of one of these.

<NumberedList>
  <NumberedItem title="It writes.">
    First drafts of quotes, proposals, emails, job adverts and reports in your tone. A person checks and sends.
  </NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="It reads.">
    It pulls details out of invoices, contracts, CVs, delivery notes and forms, then puts them where they need to go.
  </NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="It answers.">
    It replies to routine questions from customers or staff using your own price lists, policies and FAQs.
  </NumberedItem>
</NumberedList>

What these have in common is that the work follows a pattern, the inputs are written down somewhere, and a person can check the output quickly. When a task fits all three, AI is usually worth trying. When it fails one of them, for example because the rules live in one person's head, fix that first.

## A worked example

Take a made-up but typical business: an engineering supplier in Montague Gardens with 22 staff. Three people in the office handle enquiries, quotes and supplier invoices.

When they list their repetitive work, two jobs stand out. Quotes take about 40 minutes each, around 15 a week, mostly spent finding a similar old quote and checking prices. Supplier invoices take about 4 minutes each to capture, roughly 300 a month.

That's about 10 hours a week on quotes and 20 hours a month on invoices, or roughly 63 hours a month. At a loaded cost of R250 an hour for an experienced admin person, that's around R16,000 a month of time on two jobs.

The first project is the quotes, because the time saved is bigger and every hour back goes straight into following up with customers. With a tidy price list and ten good past quotes as examples, the AI drafts each quote in a couple of minutes and a person checks it in five to ten. If quotes drop from 40 minutes to 15, that frees roughly six hours a week. The invoices can come next, often with a feature already included in their accounting software.

None of these numbers are guaranteed, and your own will differ. The point is to do this sum for your business before you buy anything, because it tells you which project to start with and what "worth it" means.

## What it costs to try

Less than most people expect. If you already use Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, you probably have access to capable AI tools today, and the business plans of ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini and Claude all cost a few hundred rand per user per month. Most are billed in dollars, so the rand price moves with the exchange rate.

You also don't need a licence for everyone on day one. Start with the two or three people whose work you're changing, prove the saving, then decide who else needs one. That alone avoids the most common waste I see: [licences nobody uses](/blog/unused-chatgpt-licences).

The real cost is time. Someone has to describe the task clearly, gather examples, test the output and decide what "good enough" looks like. Budget a few hours a week for a month from whoever knows the work best. That's the part most businesses skip, and it's the part that decides whether the project works.

> If a task can be explained to a new hire in five minutes, it's a good candidate for AI.

## What about POPIA?

The moment client or staff information goes into an AI tool, POPIA applies. Pasting a client's details into a chatbot counts as processing their personal information, and so does uploading a spreadsheet. That doesn't rule AI out. It means you should know where the data goes, whether the provider trains on it, and who in your business is allowed to use which tool.

In practice, a small business covers most of this with three steps. Use business plans that don't train on your data, keep ID numbers, health and banking details out of general AI tools, and write one page of rules for staff. The [POPIA + AI checklist](/resources/popia-ai-checklist) lists the questions to answer, and the [AI usage policy template](/resources/ai-usage-policy-template) turns the answers into rules.
