<ShortAnswer>
A fractional Head of AI is a senior person who **owns your business's use of AI for a few days a month**: keeping the roadmap moving, checking new tools before you buy them, keeping the rules on data up to date and making sure staff actually use what you've paid for. It suits businesses that have started with AI and need someone to own it, but can't justify a full-time senior hire.

## The gap it fills

Most small businesses go through the same three stages with AI. First, a few people start using tools on their own. Then the owner buys some licences or a first automation. Then things drift: nobody knows who's using what, a new tool gets bought because a salesperson was persuasive, and the automation breaks in March and nobody notices until April.

The problem in the third stage isn't technology. It's that nobody owns it. The owner has a business to run, the IT provider looks after devices and security, and the person who championed the first project has their own job.

A large company would hire a Head of AI. A 40-person business can't justify that salary for what is, realistically, a few days of work a month. That's the gap a fractional role fills.

## What they actually do

The job varies by business, but most months include the same five things.

<NumberedList>
  <NumberedItem title="Keep the roadmap moving.">A short list of AI projects, in priority order, with an owner and a date for each. Each month: what moved, what's stuck and what's next.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="Check tools before you buy them.">When someone wants a new AI tool, or a vendor makes a pitch, they review it: does it solve a real problem, does it overlap with something you already pay for, and does it handle data properly?</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="Keep governance current.">The AI policy, the register of tools in use and the POPIA checks, updated as the business and the tools change. Tools change their terms often.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="Drive adoption.">Checking usage reports, running short sessions for teams that aren't using their tools, and turning one team's good prompt into everyone's.</NumberedItem>
  <NumberedItem title="Report to leadership.">A plain-English update each month: what was spent, what was saved, what's at risk and what needs a decision.</NumberedItem>
</NumberedList>

It's a leadership role, not a build role. If a project needs building, they scope it, find the right person or vendor and keep it on track. Sometimes they build small things themselves, but that's not the main job.

  <When label="Week 2">Half-day with one team, on its own work: a training session, a prompt clinic or testing a new workflow.</When>
  <When label="Week 3">Half-day on vendor and governance work: reviewing a tool someone wants, checking terms that changed, updating the policy.</When>

Between sessions, questions come in by email: "Can I use this tool for client data?" "Is this vendor any good?" Those quick answers are often where a lot of the value is, because they stop small mistakes early.

## Fractional, full-time or a consultant?

**A full-time hire** makes sense when AI is central to your product or you have enough projects to fill a senior person's week. For most businesses under 250 staff, that isn't yet the case, and the salary for someone with the right experience is a lot to commit to while you find out.

**A project consultant** is the right choice for a defined piece of work, such as an audit, a policy or a build. They finish and leave. That's good until you need someone to keep going.

**A fractional lead** sits between the two: ongoing ownership at a fraction of the cost, with the option to scale up or down. The trade-off is availability. Two days a month is two days a month, so it works best when there's someone internal who handles day-to-day questions and knows when to escalate.

## What to look for in one

The title is new and anyone can use it, so it's worth asking a few questions before you sign.

**Have they run real projects?** Look for someone who has taken AI tools from idea to everyday use in businesses like yours, not just someone who talks about AI well. Ask for an example, with what it saved.

**Are they independent?** A fractional lead who earns commission from a vendor has a reason to recommend that vendor. Ask directly whether they take referral fees.

**Do they understand POPIA?** Most of the risk in a small business's AI use is about personal information. Your lead should be comfortable with the practical side, and know when to send you to a lawyer.

**Will they tell you to stop?** Part of the job is saying no: to tools that don't fit, to projects that won't pay back and sometimes to keeping the role itself. If they'd never suggest scaling down, be cautious.

**Is the scope written down?** Days per month, what's included and how to change it, agreed at a fixed fee. Vague arrangements drift.

## When you don't need one

Be wary of anyone selling you a fractional role too early. You probably don't need one if:

<Checklist>
  <li>You haven't started using AI yet. An audit or roadmap comes first, so there's something to own.</li>
  <li>You have one tool and one use case, and it's working.</li>
  <li>Someone in the business already owns it and has the time and seniority to do it well.</li>
</Checklist>

In those cases a one-off project, or a few hours of advice when you need it, is better value.

## A worked example

Picture a made-up logistics company in Gqeberha with 70 staff. Over the past year it bought ChatGPT Business for the office, an AI tool for capturing waybills and a customer-query chatbot. Each was a good idea. Together they cost a fair amount every month, and nobody was sure how much any of them were used.

A fractional Head of AI, at two days a month, starts by pulling the usage reports. The chatbot is working well. The capture tool is used by one of three depots, because the other two were never set up. Half the ChatGPT seats are unused.

In the first three months, the other depots get set up on the capture tool. Unused seats are cancelled and the rest of the team gets a short session on their own tasks. A vendor pitching a route-planning AI gets a proper review, and the company decides to wait a year. The policy is updated because the chatbot provider changed its data terms.

None of this was difficult. It just needed someone whose job it was.
